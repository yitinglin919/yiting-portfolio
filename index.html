Virtual Intimacy, Real Communities

Two multimedia stories exploring how romance game fandom shapes intimacy and community beyond the screen.

About the project

From commissioned character performances to fan-organised birthday celebrations, these stories examine how people transform their emotional connections with fictional characters into real-world experiences, relationships and communities.

My role: Story development, reporting, interviews, photography, and video production.

01 One-Day Romance

The 'fantasy date' trend Chinese women are embracing

Photos: supplied

Summary

As otome commission culture rapidly grew in 2025, I began following the relationships between players and cosplayers who take on commissioned dates. As an otome game player myself, I was interested in one question: when someone knows the character is fictional, what are they emotionally responding to — the character, the performer, or something in between?

02 He's Not Real, But His Birthday Is

Searching for Xavier: A fictitious birthday is celebrated in real life

Summary

In 2026, I contacted the organiser of a local otome fan club to document a birthday celebration for Xavier, a fictional character from Love and Deepspace.

Most attendees preferred not to show their faces, so filming required careful attention to their privacy. Nevertheless, they welcomed me into the celebration.

I documented fans exchanging handmade gifts and interacting with a cosplayer portraying Xavier, exploring how affection for a fictional character could bring strangers together and create meaningful connections in real life.

Across both stories

Reflections

I began by questioning why affection for fictional romantic characters could lead people to hire performers for dates, and where the boundaries between intimacy, fantasy and commerce might lie.

Through interviews, I recognised something more fundamental: the desire to be seen, heard and valued.

At a character birthday celebration, I watched fans exchange handmade gifts, a cosplayer become captivating through the audience's affection, and a hesitant participant join a group photograph with encouragement. These moments revealed how real human connections can grow around fictional characters.

Yet a contradiction remains. While fans create their own experiences and communities, their devotion may also strengthen the characters' commercial value.

Can the same love that makes a fictional character feel more real also deepen their identity as a commodity?

Home away from home

Summary

Part of a wider series on ageing, this story looks at the options available to Chinese older people living in New Zealand.

When I entered the aged-care home, I was struck by how familiar the space felt. Chinese magazines, toys, kitchenware and small objects brought across countries had turned individual rooms into fragments of home. I began photographing these details and the lives built around them.

My role: Photography and video production.

Read the published story

Video

About the Project

This was not a story I originally pitched. After completing the planned interviews with the reporter, I asked residents for permission to spend more time in their rooms and photograph the spaces around them.

Objects became traces of memory and migration. One resident played old Chinese pop songs from his computer and spoke about the beauty — and loneliness — of living here, before quickly insisting that he was not lonely at all.

I moved through their everyday rhythms: meals, Chinese television, Hong Kong news, conversations and quiet time. What stayed with me was the way they had created a small world of familiarity far from home — a place containing both solitude and companionship.
